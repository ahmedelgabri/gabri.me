Last month [Thorsten Ball](https://x.com/thorstenball) tweeted this And a couple
of days ago [Mitchell Hashimoto](https://x.com/mitchellh) shared how his team
works at Superlogical. There is also how
[Claude Code team does reviews](https://claude.dev/blog/solving-code-review-at-anthropic/),
[Satya Nadella talking about how team sizes will change](https://x.com/Vvikramai/status/2122362551568843218),
[Clare Liguori, talking about pilots in Amazon](https://youtu.be/pqlWNihgdjI?si=32644fcxJTss2NkH)
and more examples [^1].

And it's not only them. Everyone is talking about software engineering in the
agentic era, how teams should ship code and how the whole software development
lifecycle is changing. Some patterns keep showing up, like pushing to master, no
code reviews and very light processes. [^2]

I've been thinking about this for a while, and every time I read one of these I
have the same reaction. I've seen this before.

## None of this is actually new

Most of these patterns were already there in the best teams I worked with, years
before anyone had an agent writing code for them. Here is what these teams
looked like:

- Small. Coincidentally _or not_ they were always around four or five people
  max.
- **Very** low on processes and very high on automation. CI, linting and
  automations did the boring checks so people didn't have to.
- PR reviews were just another pair of eyes. It was not about nitpicking, and it
  was not about "knowledge sharing" either. We still did reviews, so "no code
  reviews" is the one pattern from that list we didn't have. But a review was
  never a gate, and that's why dropping it is a small step for teams like these.
- Ownership was shared. Nobody cared about who owns what, we cared about what we
  were building as a whole. If you saw something broken, you fixed it.
- High trust and high agency. Most of the time we didn't need a PM or an EM to
  tell us what to do next.
- High impact. These were always among the most productive teams in the
  organization, and everyone else could see it.

About that last point, I remember one engineering manager we had in one of these
teams. He just came and said "You handle the work, I'll handle all the
processes. No one needs to touch Jira and stuff, I'll fill all of this for you.
Just focus on the work."

## So where did all the process come from?

I also worked in teams where PRs were meant for "knowledge sharing", and where
every change needed an approval from the team that "owns" the code. When I think
about it, this only happened in two cases:

1. The team grew too much, which multiplied the communication paths between
   people and made it hard to keep the context shared.
2. The organization overhired and had too many teams owning tiny pieces, so code
   ownership became a problem and then a gatekeeping mechanism.

In both cases the review stopped being another pair of eyes and became a gate.
Instead of "did I miss something?" it became "am I allowed to change this?"

Of course there are other cases too, where the organization is stuck in the 80s
or something and just wants to micromanage everyone.

## What did agents change then?

Agents are multipliers, they multiply whatever you give them. So give them to a
small, high trust team and that team ships a lot more than before. And vice
versa, give them to a team where every change waits for an approval or a
process, and you will only see the gap between these two teams getting bigger.

And I think this is the actual change. These well functioning teams always had
the right balance between process, trust, agency and action. Everyone could see
the results, but not how the team got them, because that part often meant
quietly ignoring the rules. Funny enough, everyone used to ask them "how can you
ship so fast?" or "your team is one of the most productive teams, how?"

There was no secret. _Well_, the secret was that the team optimized everything,
every single detail, to work for them and not against them. And they were all
aligned on that.

The funny part was if you got an EM or PM who was not aligned, it quickly showed
in the team output and that EM or PM automatically had to adapt to the team.

Organizations always optimize for the average case, they don't take the outliers
into consideration. But here is the thing, most of them don't care if you break
the rules as long as you are shipping and helping the business.

Now with agents these teams don't need to hide how they work anymore. They can
prove that this actually works, and everyone is starting to move in that
direction.

## The future

The future looks a lot like the past. Small teams of four or five people max,
high trust, high agency and shared ownership. Low on process, high on automation
and guardrails.

Organizations and teams that don't act on this now will be left behind. No,
actually, they will be out of business.

Just look at the amount of large scale rewrites/migrations/work happening around
you, these were not even a topic for discussion two years ago.

- [Amazon Prime: 90 weeks project down to 24 weeks (6 engineers)](https://youtu.be/pqlWNihgdjI?si=SJxeaph3naB6GlQe&t=246)
- [Amazon Bedrock: 18-month project in 76 days (6 engineers)](https://youtu.be/pqlWNihgdjI?si=w-uvSzyDNMDPcwKh&t=144)
- [Bun: Zig -> Rust](https://bun.com/blog/bun-in-rust)
- [Uber: JUnit migration](https://www.uber.com/us/en/blog/junit-migration/)
- [Max Schwenk porting TypeScript Go to Rust (tsrs)](https://blog.cloudflare.com/typescript-rust-port/)
- [Airbnb: 3.5K tests from Enzyme to React Testing Library](https://airbnb.tech/infrastructure/accelerating-large-scale-test-migration-with-llms/)
- [Asana: Enzyme to React Testing Library](https://asana.com/inside-asana/migrating-off-enzyme-2-weeks)

People are being cynical about these and labeling these types of migrations as
"slop" or "we need to rewrite everything in Rust now", but they are not looking
at the bigger picture. What was not possible two years ago, and used to take
months or years to be done, is now VERY possible and takes only weeks, with much
smaller teams or even single individuals. Which changes the game completely!

These migrations didn't succeed because of agents, they succeeded because of the
guardrails and automation around them. Agents just made them possible. Each one
had a test suite to tell the agent when it was wrong, the same kind of
automation the teams above always relied on.

## So should everyone just push to master?

No! And that's what most people miss.

Pushing to master works for these teams because of everything else they have.
They trust each other, and their automation catches what a reviewer would have
caught. If you only copy the "no reviews" part, you will ship bugs faster and
that's it.

So there are steps you have to do before that. Fix your CI/CD, automate your
guardrails, improve your processes, encode your steps into skills and improve
your context.
[Amazon has a list of how to become a frontier team](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/machine-learning/how-frontier-teams-are-reinventing-ai-native-development/).

Evaluate your situation, understand the risk and the cost for you, and then
decide. Either you do it now and fix things while you are moving, or you fix
first and do it later.

Agents don't fix taste either. If an agent can write a thousand lines in ten
minutes, someone still needs to know which thousand lines are worth writing.
That was always the hard part, it's only more visible now. Ben Kuhn has a
[great post](https://www.benkuhn.net/impact/) on agency and taste.

So yes, everything changes. The tools, the speed, how much code one person can
ship. But what makes a team good didn't change at all. Small teams, high trust,
high agency, low process and great taste in what they are building.

For me this doesn't feel like a new way of working. It feels like going back to
the best teams I've worked with.
